Correcting the Most Common Swing Flaw

There's one flaw I see more than any other, in golfers of every level: pulling the arms in toward the body instead of extending them out toward the target. It sounds small. It isn't. That single habit is behind more slices, pulled shots, and lost distance than anything else I work on with students.

Think about a hockey player taking a slap shot, a quarterback throwing a long pass, or a hitter swinging for the fences. What do all three have in common? Full extension. The arms reach away from the body toward the target, and that extension is what drives the body's weight forward. Most golfers never do this in their swing. Instead, they pull the club in tight to the body, and that single habit creates a chain reaction: the spine straightens and the head lifts, the body can't move forward because the arms are pulling the weight backward, and balance suffers — sometimes badly enough that the lead leg has to step out just to stay upright. Nobody swings a baseball bat that way, and golf shouldn't be any different.

With a longer club, that inward pull keeps the face from closing and sends the ball slicing off to the right. With a shorter club, like a 9-iron, it causes its own version of the same miss. Either way, the root cause is the same: no extension.

The chair drill

Here's one of my favorite drills for fixing this. Set a chair just to the right of your target line, with the leg nearest you resting right on that line. Take your stance further back than usual — far enough that you'll need to reach a little to get the clubhead to the chair.

Now, in slow motion, make a half swing — gently, since we're not trying to damage the chair or the club — and swing the club under the chair, between your legs, with the toe of the club pointing toward the target. If that feels difficult, that's usually a sign the toe of the club isn't actually pointing at the target yet.

Hold that position and notice what's happening: your arms should be fully extended, and your weight should have shifted onto your front foot. The first few times through, that weight shift can feel unfamiliar enough that you feel like you're going to fall forward. That's a good sign — it means you're finally extending the way you're supposed to.
